# Exam prep: retrieval beats rereading, and the audit comes first

The dashboard's job at exam time is to turn the accumulated trail (filed materials,
debriefs, your notes, the professor's decks) into preparation that actually transfers.
Two principles carry everything else.

**Retrieval beats exposition.** Rereading a well-made guide feels like studying and
mostly isn't — recognition is not recall. Once material has been read once, prep should
be self-test questions, blank-page prompts ("draw the framework and label every part"),
and explain-it-back exercises. Build answer keys, but keep them physically separate from
prompts so the eye can't cheat. Tag drills by topic and difficulty so a weak area can be
drilled selectively, and interleave topics rather than blocking by chapter.

**Audit coverage at the START of prep, not the eve.** The first act of exam prep is
diffing your prepared material against the instructor's announced scope (topic list,
review deck, sample exam). Every gap found on day one is a study item; every gap found
the night before is a panic. The original learned this by missing an announced topic
until the last evening — once was enough.

Other rules that earned their place:

- **Read sources in this order**: the instructor's review materials, your own submitted
  work (it carries verified facts in your own words), the professor's post-class decks,
  and only then the raw readings. Your own graded submissions are the most underrated
  study asset you have.
- **Standalone or it's broken.** Any study sheet must be readable with *nothing else
  open*: every number derived or introduced on the page, every term defined at first
  touch, every attribution checked against the actual course materials. A plausible
  "fact" the course never taught is worse than a gap, because it gets studied.
- **Cheat sheets are not shorthand.** Where a formula sheet is allowed, one formula per
  line, set large, with a plain-English gloss under each naming every symbol. A
  "space-efficient" sheet packed with fragments and arrows proved unusable under exam
  stress. White space beats content breadth; check the instructor's sheet rules before
  designing.
- **Closed-universe exams get segregated sources.** For case exams, the only citable
  facts are the handed-out pages. Keep any outside research about the real company in a
  separate, loudly-labeled file so the two can never blur under pressure.
- **Figures show mechanisms** — a curve, a flow with a bottleneck, a spectrum — and get
  numbered, captioned, and legended. A list dressed as a diagram is decoration.

## Build sketch

Start with a per-exam page assembled by your agent: announced scope, the coverage audit
result, links to the trail (debriefs, decks, submissions), and a growing bank of drills
generated from the professor's own posted problems. Generate drills into the page, not
into chat — a drill you can revisit beats a conversation that scrolls away.
